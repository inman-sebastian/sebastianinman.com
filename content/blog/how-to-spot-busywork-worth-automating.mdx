Every owner I talk to has a list of tasks they hate. The surprise is that the most hated task usually isn't the best one to automate. Hate is a bad measuring stick; some annoying jobs happen twice a year and take ten minutes. The right measuring stick is simpler, and you can apply it yourself in about five minutes.

## The three questions

Run any task through these:

<CheckList items={[
  "Does it happen every week, or every day? Frequency is where the hours hide.",
  "Does it work the same way every time? If you could write the steps on an index card for a new hire, a system can follow those steps too.",
  "Does anything bad happen when it slips? Missed follow-ups, late invoices, and unanswered questions cost real money. Tasks that quietly forgive you when you forget them are lower stakes.",
]} />

Two yeses make a task worth a look. Three yeses and it's probably costing you more than automating it would.

## What that looks like in practice

Take chasing late invoices. It happens every week, it works exactly the same way every time (wait, check, remind, repeat), and when it slips, you don't get paid. Three for three. That's why payment reminders are usually the first thing I set up for a new client, and why they tend to pay for themselves fast.

Now take something like writing your holiday hours post. Annoying? Sure. But it happens a few times a year, takes twenty minutes, and nothing breaks if it's a day late. Automating it would be building a machine to save you an hour a year. Skip it.

<Callout title="The honest version of this advice">
  Some tasks fail the test and should stay manual. Anyone who tells you
  everything in your business should be automated is selling something.
  The goal is fewer hours lost, not more software.
</Callout>

## The five-minute exercise

Grab whatever's nearby and list the five tasks that ate your last week. Run each through the three questions. Most owners find one or two clear winners, and that's plenty; a single well-chosen automation usually matters more than five mediocre ones.
