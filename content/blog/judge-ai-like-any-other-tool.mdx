## Two loud voices, both selling something

One says AI is coming for your job, your staff, and your trade by
Christmas. The other says it will transform your business, and the
transformation starts with a credit card and a free trial.

Neither of those is describing anything you can actually do on Tuesday
morning. They are both easier to sell than the boring version, which is
that AI is a tool. Some of it is genuinely useful in a small business.
Plenty of it is not worth what it costs. Telling those apart is a skill
you already have, and you have used it before.

## You have done this before

Think about the last piece of kit that came into the business. A card
reader. An online booking system. The accounting software you moved to
when the shoebox stopped being funny.

You never needed to know how a card network settles a payment overnight.
You needed to know what the thing did, what it cost, whether it would
break, and how annoyed you would be if it did. That was the right way to
think about it then, and nothing about the current noise changes it.

## The questions that still work

<CheckList items={[
  "What job would this do, in one sentence? If nobody can answer without saying 'leverage' or 'transform', it is not ready for your money.",
  "What does it cost, all in, including the month after the free trial ends?",
  "What happens when it gets something wrong, and would I find out?",
  "If I stop paying, do I still have my customer list, my bookings, my numbers?",
  "Who like me is already using it? Not a wall of logos for companies fifty times the size."
]} />

Not one of those is a question about artificial intelligence. That is
rather the point. A tool that cannot survive ordinary questions was
never going to survive contact with your Saturday.

## Where the comparison breaks down

Being fair about this means saying where it stops being just another
gadget. Two things really are different, and they are worth knowing.

<Callout title="The two that matter">
**It can be wrong and sound completely sure of itself.** A calculator
that broke would give you obvious nonsense. This gives you a confident,
well-written, wrong answer in a pleasant tone. Which makes it good for a
first draft and a poor choice for the last word on anything with money
or a legal date attached.

**What you type into it goes somewhere.** Before you paste a customer
list, a contract, or anything with a person's details in it, go and find
the setting that says whether your input gets used to train the thing.
It is usually there, it is often switched on when you arrive, and it is
usually worth switching off.
</Callout>

## The honest position

You do not need an opinion about artificial intelligence. You need to
know whether a specific thing saves you a specific number of hours for a
price you would pay. That is a much smaller question, and you are better
at answering it than anyone selling you the big one.

If the answer is no, the answer is no. A tool you would use twice a year
is not worth setting up, whatever is powering it underneath. I would
rather tell you that on a phone call than sell you something that sits
there.
